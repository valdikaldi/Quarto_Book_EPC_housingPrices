7. Discussion

7. Conclusion and Discussion

This thesis set out to investigate whether home buyers are inattentive towards energy efficiency by utilising a unique and comprehensive dataset. The dataset was constructed from scratch by extracting and merging information from 776,106 EPC reports and more than 2.2 million property transactions. The main dataset consists of 728,794 residential property transactions in Denmark spanning from 1 July 2010 to 2023. Through the extraction and processing of information using regular expressions, OCR, and NLP techniques, and the subsequent merging with housing data, we have created a detailed dataset that allows for a multifaceted analysis of the relationship between energy efficiency and property prices. This enables us to account for a broader range of property characteristics, geographical factors, and information contained in EPC reports than is typically considered in the empirical analysis presented in the literature reviewed in this thesis.

The primary contribution of this study is threefold. First, unlike previous studies, which, to the best of our knowledge, have primarily focused on detached houses, this study broadens the analysis to include detached houses, row houses, duplexes, and apartments. This provides a more comprehensive perspective on how energy efficiency is valued across different segments of the housing market. Second, our study goes beyond EPC labels and estimated energy consumption by incorporating additional information from EPC reports concerning the potential for energy-efficient renovations. This allows us to investigate whether home buyers value not only the current energy efficiency of a property but also its potential for future improvements. Third, we examine whether the relationship between energy efficiency and property prices changed following the introduction of tighter energy-efficiency requirements in January 2018.

Main Findings

The results provide evidence that energy performance is an important factor in the valuation of residential properties in Denmark. Across the main specifications, properties with more energy-efficient EPC labels generally command higher prices than properties with label D, while properties with less energy-efficient labels generally sell at discounts. In Model 4, for example, properties labelled A2020, A2015, and A2010 command estimated premiums of 18.6%, 16.4%, and 11.3%, respectively, relative to label D. Properties labelled B and C command premiums of 7.6% and 4.7%, while properties labelled E, F, and G are associated with discounts of 7.9%, 15.8%, and 36.5%, respectively.

However, the estimated label effects become substantially smaller when additional information on renovation potential and more detailed time-by-geography fixed effects are introduced. In the final specification, the premiums associated with A2020, A2015, and A2010 decrease to 7.7%, 6.4%, and 2.6%, respectively, while the premiums for B and C are 2.2% and 2.0%. At the same time, the discounts associated with E, F, and G remain sizeable at 6.2%, 12.5%, and 28.7%, respectively.

This reduction in the EPC label effects is an important finding for the interpretation of the results. It suggests that part of the relationship between EPC labels and property prices may be related to characteristics that are correlated with energy efficiency and are not captured when considering the EPC label alone. In particular, the results indicate that renovation potential contains relevant information for property valuation. Consequently, estimates that focus only on EPC labels may overstate the extent to which the label itself captures the value placed on energy efficiency.

The findings therefore provide a more nuanced answer to the question of whether home buyers are inattentive towards energy efficiency. The results do not suggest that home buyers are generally inattentive to energy efficiency. Instead, they suggest that buyers respond to several dimensions of energy efficiency, but that these dimensions are not valued equally.

Energy Efficiency Beyond the EPC Label

An important finding of this thesis is that home buyers appear to value the potential for future improvements in energy efficiency. When renovation characteristics are incorporated into the final specification, the potential energy label resulting from implementing the renovation suggestions has a significant relationship with property prices. The relationship is also non-linear. A one-label improvement in potential energy efficiency has a relatively small positive effect, while greater potential initially increases the price effect before the relationship becomes negative for sufficiently large potential improvements.

This pattern suggests that renovation potential may contain two opposing signals for potential buyers. On the one hand, the possibility of improving a property’s energy efficiency may represent an opportunity to increase the property’s quality and reduce future energy consumption. On the other hand, a large improvement potential may also signal that the property currently requires substantial renovation. Thus, a high potential EPC improvement may simultaneously represent an opportunity and an indication of the investment required to realise that opportunity.

The results for the estimated costs and benefits of renovations provide further support for this interpretation. The estimated total energy cost saved and the estimated investment cost associated with profitable renovations have relatively modest and inelastic effects on property prices. This suggests that home buyers may not place the same weight on the generalised monetary estimates provided in the EPC reports as they do on more tangible characteristics of the property and its renovation potential.

This distinction is important when considering the question of inattention. The findings do not necessarily indicate that buyers ignore energy-related information. Instead, they may indicate that buyers place greater weight on information that is easier to relate to the physical condition and characteristics of the property than on generalised estimates of future monetary savings and renovation costs.

The results for specific renovation recommendations reinforce this interpretation. For example, properties for which an upgrade to district heating is suggested are associated with a 7.8% higher price, whereas properties with recommendations concerning windows or doors are associated with a 5.3% lower price. These results suggest that the type of renovation matters, rather than renovation potential being valued uniformly across all possible improvements.

Non-linear Effects of Energy Efficiency

The results also provide evidence of a potential non-linear relationship between energy efficiency and property prices. This pattern emerges both in the analysis across property types and in the robustness tests.

Across property types, detached houses generally exhibit the largest premiums for highly energy-efficient EPC labels. Compared with label D, detached houses with A2020, A2015, A2010, and B labels are associated with premiums of 15.2%, 16.6%, 9.9%, and 8.7%, respectively. The corresponding premiums are considerably smaller for apartments. For example, the estimated premiums for A2020, A2015, and B apartments are all approximately 3.7%, while the premium for A2010 is 5.4%.

The pattern across labels also suggests diminishing returns to increasingly high energy efficiency. In several specifications, the most energy-efficient label does not necessarily command a larger premium than the immediately preceding A label. This is particularly visible in the robustness tests, where A2020 does not consistently outperform A2015. Similarly, the property-type analysis indicates that premiums may decline once a property reaches the highest levels of energy efficiency.

One possible interpretation is that moving from a relatively inefficient property towards a high level of energy efficiency provides substantial perceived benefits, while further improvements beyond an already high level of efficiency provide increasingly smaller additional benefits. The results are therefore consistent with diminishing marginal valuation of energy efficiency. However, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of a potential non-linear relationship rather than as definitive evidence of a specific behavioural mechanism.

Differences Across Property Types

The analysis of different property types demonstrates that the value associated with energy efficiency is not uniform across the housing market. Detached houses generally display the strongest relationship between EPC labels and property prices, whereas the effects for apartments are considerably smaller. The results for duplexes are less precise, which is likely related to the substantially smaller sample of 8,236 observations. Consequently, the results for duplexes should be interpreted with greater caution.

The difference between detached houses and apartments may partly reflect the way energy-related investments and costs are distributed. For detached houses, renovation decisions, energy consumption, and associated costs are more directly connected to the individual household. In apartment buildings, by contrast, some energy-related characteristics and renovation costs are assessed at the building level and are therefore shared across multiple units.

This distinction is particularly relevant for the estimated energy costs and investment costs in the EPC reports. For detached houses, these estimates relate directly to the individual property, whereas for apartments they can relate to the entire apartment block. Consequently, the monetary benefits and costs associated with energy-efficiency improvements may be perceived differently by buyers of apartments and detached houses.

The property-type results therefore suggest that the same EPC information does not necessarily have the same economic meaning across different housing-market segments. Energy efficiency may be more directly observable and actionable for buyers of detached houses, while the collective nature of apartment buildings may weaken the relationship between individual EPC characteristics and property prices.

At the same time, these explanations should be treated as potential mechanisms rather than established causal explanations. The analysis identifies differences in estimated effects across property types, but further statistical testing would be required to establish whether the coefficients are statistically different from one another and to identify the mechanisms behind these differences.

Energy Sources and Systems

The results also show that home buyers respond to the energy source and heating systems of a property in addition to the EPC label itself. District heating is generally associated with relatively favourable prices, while properties relying on fossil fuels, particularly oil, tend to be associated with lower prices.

In the main specification, properties using natural gas, oil, or a combination of electricity and oil are associated with discounts relative to district heating, while some combinations involving electricity are associated with premiums. The property-type analysis further shows that the value associated with different energy sources varies substantially across housing segments.

The results for specific energy systems are also informative. Solar cells, solar heating, and heat pumps are generally associated with positive price effects in the main analysis, although the magnitude and statistical significance vary across property types and specifications. In particular, the effect of heat pumps is less stable than the effects of solar cells and solar heating.

The robustness analysis provides additional support for the positive association between these systems and property prices. Increasing the classification threshold for the NLP-based variables does not substantially alter the coefficients of the other key variables, while the coefficients for solar cells and solar heating remain positive and statistically significant. The heat-pump coefficient remains positive but exhibits greater variation in statistical significance.

Taken together, these results suggest that home buyers appear to respond to tangible energy-related characteristics of properties. However, the variation across energy sources and systems indicates that energy efficiency is not valued as a single homogeneous characteristic.

The 2018 Policy Change

The analysis of the period before and after the implementation of the 2018 policy provides evidence of a change in the relationship between energy efficiency and property prices. Before the policy, A2020 was negative and statistically insignificant, while A2010 and B were significant only at the 10% level. Following the implementation of the policy, A2020 is associated with a 6.2% premium relative to label D, and the other energy-efficient labels, with the exception of A2015, experience increases in their estimated premiums.

The results also indicate a stronger preference for district heating following the policy implementation. Discounts associated with oil and the combination of oil and electricity increased from 9.3% to 12.5% and from 10.7% to 14.8%, respectively. At the same time, the estimated premiums associated with heat pumps, solar cells, and solar heating declined.

The analysis also suggests that the value associated with energy-efficiency potential increased after the policy was implemented, while changes in the types of renovations valued by consumers were relatively small.

Taken together, these results are consistent with a shift in the relationship between energy efficiency and property prices following the implementation of the policy. In particular, the results suggest greater valuation of higher EPC performance, energy-efficiency potential, and district heating.

However, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that the 2018 policy itself caused the observed changes. The post-policy period contains several other important changes that could potentially affect the housing market and the valuation of energy efficiency. In particular, the period following the policy implementation includes the COVID-19 pandemic and the energy price shock following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. Therefore, the observed differences between the pre- and post-policy periods may reflect a combination of the policy and other changes occurring over the same period.

A more detailed identification strategy would therefore be required to isolate the causal effect of the policy from these other developments.

Robustness of the Findings

The robustness tests provide overall support for the main findings. Removing observations based on transaction prices, price per square metre, and selected property characteristics does not substantially change the signs or statistical significance of the key variables. This suggests that the main relationship between EPC characteristics and property prices is not driven solely by the particular observations removed in these specifications.

The use of estimated energy consumption as an alternative measure of energy efficiency provides further support for the main results. Energy consumption has a negative and statistically significant relationship with property prices, with a 1% increase in estimated energy consumption associated with a 9.7% decrease in property prices. This is consistent with the broader finding that lower energy consumption and greater energy efficiency are associated with higher property prices.

However, the energy-consumption analysis is based on a substantially smaller sample of 582,690 observations because estimated energy consumption could not be extracted for all properties. This limitation should therefore be considered when interpreting the result.

The time-on-the-market analysis also supports the main findings. Energy-efficient properties sell faster than D-labelled properties, while less energy-efficient properties take longer to sell. Interestingly, A2010 properties sell the fastest, followed by A2020 and A2015 properties. This ordering again provides evidence consistent with a non-linear relationship between energy efficiency and housing-market outcomes.

Taken together, the robustness tests suggest that the main findings are not solely dependent on the precise specification of the dependent variable, the treatment of potential outliers, or the classification thresholds used for the NLP-derived variables. Nevertheless, each robustness test has its own limitations, and the results should therefore be viewed as complementary evidence rather than as definitive proof of a single underlying mechanism.

Implications for the Research Question

The central question of this thesis is whether home buyers are inattentive towards energy efficiency. Based on the results, the answer appears to be more nuanced than a simple yes or no.

The results provide clear evidence that energy efficiency is reflected in property prices. Energy-efficient EPC labels are generally associated with higher prices, while inefficient labels are associated with lower prices. Home buyers also appear to value specific energy sources and systems, as well as the potential for future improvements in energy efficiency.

At the same time, the magnitude of the EPC label effect changes considerably once renovation potential and more detailed fixed effects are incorporated. Furthermore, the estimated monetary benefits from future energy savings and the investment costs of profitable renovations have relatively modest and inelastic effects. These findings suggest that home buyers do not necessarily treat all energy-related information in the EPC reports equally.

Thus, rather than characterising home buyers as simply inattentive, the results suggest that their valuation of energy efficiency may be selective. Buyers appear to place value on observable EPC labels, specific energy systems, and renovation potential, while the estimated monetary implications of future energy savings and renovation expenditures appear to receive less weight.

This distinction is important. If buyers were entirely inattentive to energy efficiency, we would not expect such consistent relationships between EPC labels, energy systems, renovation potential, and property prices. Conversely, the reduction in the EPC label effects after controlling for renovation potential indicates that the label alone should not be interpreted as a complete measure of how buyers value energy efficiency.

The findings therefore suggest that the information contained in EPC reports may affect property valuation through several different channels. The current energy performance of the property, the type of energy system, and the possibility of future improvements appear to matter, but their effects differ in magnitude and across property types. The results consequently point towards a more complex relationship between information, perceived property quality, renovation requirements, and energy efficiency than can be captured by EPC labels alone.

Limitations and Further Research

Despite the comprehensive nature of the dataset and our efforts to account for a wide range of property characteristics and geographical factors, we cannot rule out the possibility of omitted-variable bias. Factors such as the view from a property, local school quality, and crime rates may influence property prices but are not fully accounted for in the analysis. The inclusion of high-dimensional fixed effects helps account for some unobserved geographical and temporal heterogeneity, but it cannot eliminate all potential sources of omitted-variable bias.

Furthermore, the hedonic price framework does not allow us to fully disentangle supply- and demand-side effects. The estimated relationships therefore describe how energy-related characteristics are reflected in transaction prices, but they do not necessarily identify the separate contributions of buyers’ willingness to pay and sellers’ decisions to supply properties with particular characteristics.

The results also highlight several areas for further investigation. First, the non-linear relationship between EPC labels and property prices warrants further analysis. In particular, the finding that A2020 does not consistently command a higher premium than A2015 suggests that the marginal value of increasingly high energy efficiency may decline.

Second, the differences between detached houses and apartments deserve further investigation. The building-level nature of energy-cost and investment estimates for apartments may contribute to the smaller effects observed in this market segment. However, further analysis is needed to determine whether these differences are statistically significant and to identify the mechanisms underlying them.

Third, the robustness results indicate that the heat-pump coefficient is less stable than those of solar cells and solar heating. Further analysis distinguishing between different types of heat pumps could therefore provide a more detailed understanding of how these systems are valued.

Finally, the distribution of EPC labels in the sample raises an additional data-related concern. As shown in Figure ?fig-epc-population-vs-sample in ?sec-appendix-additional-figures, the sample contains relatively few observations for label A2015. The sample therefore does not appear to fully align with the population distribution of the three A labels. This discrepancy may affect the interpretation of comparisons between the A-label categories and warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

Overall, this thesis provides evidence that energy efficiency is reflected in Danish residential property prices, but that its valuation is more complex than a simple relationship between EPC labels and transaction prices. Home buyers appear to value current energy performance, specific energy sources and systems, and the potential for future improvements. However, the substantially smaller EPC label effects after accounting for renovation potential indicate that part of the observed label premium may reflect other characteristics associated with energy efficiency.

The results further suggest that the relationship between energy efficiency and property prices may be non-linear, with diminishing returns at higher levels of energy efficiency. This pattern is observed across property types and is further supported by the robustness analysis using time on the market and alternative specifications.

The findings therefore do not support the conclusion that home buyers are simply inattentive towards energy efficiency. Instead, they suggest that buyers appear to pay attention to energy efficiency, but selectively. Tangible and readily observable characteristics of energy performance appear to matter more than generalised estimates of future energy savings and renovation costs. Moreover, the importance of these characteristics differs substantially across property types.

The 2018 policy analysis provides evidence of a change in the valuation of energy efficiency following the policy’s implementation, including greater premiums for several energy-efficient labels, greater valuation of energy-efficiency potential, and a stronger preference for district heating. However, given the other major developments occurring during the post-policy period, these results should be interpreted as changes associated with the post-policy period rather than as definitive causal effects of the policy.

Taken together, the results demonstrate the value of extracting information beyond the EPC label itself. The additional information contained in EPC reports provides important insights into how energy efficiency is incorporated into property prices and suggests that understanding the housing-market value of energy efficiency requires consideration of both the current state of a property and its potential future improvements. The unique dataset developed in this thesis provides a foundation for further research into these relationships and for more detailed investigation of how home buyers perceive and value energy-efficiency information.
