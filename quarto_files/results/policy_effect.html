6.3 Policy Effect

Denmark introduced a policy on January 1, 2018, that imposed greater energy-efficiency restrictions for new buildings as well as tighter renovation standards for existing properties. The objective of this policy is to promote the development of energy-efficient properties and ensure that renovations made to existing properties improve overall energy performance. In this chapter, we explore whether the implementation of this policy has influenced home buyers’ perceptions of energy-efficient properties and their potential for energy efficiency.

Following the empirical strategy outlined by Jensen et al. (2016), we divide our sample according to the policy implementation date. Model 1 contains observations from before the policy was implemented, whereas Model 2 includes observations from after the policy was implemented. Both models represent Equation 7.2. Notably, before the implementation of the policy, label A2020 is found to have a negative but statistically insignificant effect, while labels A2010 and B are significant only at the 10% level. Additionally, label A2020, which was initially negative and statistically insignificant, gains a price premium of 6.2% compared to label D after the policy was implemented. Before the policy, the highest premium was found for properties labelled A2015, with a premium of 5.1% compared to label D. However, after the policy’s implementation, all energy-efficient labels, excluding A2015, experienced a significant increase in price premiums. The premium for label A2015 decreased, likely due to a stronger push towards higher-tier energy-efficient labels after the policy was enacted.

In addition to changes in the label effects, we also observe a significant change in home buyers’ perceptions of energy sources. While some energy sources experienced only marginal changes, others exhibited more substantial shifts. For instance, compared to district heating, properties heated solely with oil or with a combination of oil and electricity were sold at average discounts of 9.3% and 10.7%, respectively, before the policy. After the policy’s implementation, these discounts increased to 12.5% and 14.8%, respectively. At the same time, the price premiums associated with green energy systems, including heat pumps, solar cells, and solar heating, declined.

Overall, these results indicate that the implementation of the policy has not only shifted home buyers’ preferences towards more energy-efficient properties but also towards greater consideration of green energy sources, particularly district heating. However, this shift appears to have reduced the attention given to other green energy components, such as heat pumps, solar cells, and solar heating.

The policy also significantly affected the perceived energy-efficiency potential of properties. Following the policy’s implementation, home buyers placed greater value on the energy-efficiency potential of properties, resulting in higher price premiums for properties with greater potential to improve their EPC labels. While there were some changes in the types of renovations valued by consumers, these changes were relatively minor or negligible.

The results indicate that, following the implementation of the policy, home buyers’ preferences and market dynamics shifted in favour of energy efficiency. The policy coincided with a clear trend towards greater value being placed on energy-efficient properties, particularly those with higher EPC labels and greater potential for energy savings.

Jensen, Ole Michael, Anders Rhiger Hansen, and Jesper Kragh. 2016. “Market Response to the Public Display of Energy Performance Rating at Property Sales.” Energy Policy 93 (June): 229–35. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2016.02.029.
